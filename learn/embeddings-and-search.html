Embeddings and search: finding meaning, not just matching words.

How can a computer know that “people giving their time” is about volunteering, even though the word never appears? Embeddings make this possible. They are used in semantic search and RAG.

  • About 25 minutes
  • Intermediate
  • No account required
  • Original diagrams

Meaning as a location

An embedding turns a piece of text into a list of numbers, representing a point in a “meaning space”. An embedding model is trained so that texts with similar meanings land close together, even when they use completely different words.

Once text is a point, searching becomes geometry: turn the question into a point too, and find the nearest neighbours. This is called semantic search, and it is the engine inside most “chat with your documents” tools.

About this lab: real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of dimensions and are learned by a model. Ours have just five hand-labelled dimensions: volunteering, money, research, health and events, so you can see exactly what is going on. In real embeddings, individual dimensions don't have human-readable names like these: meaning is spread across all of them. The similarity calculations are real.

        Key ideas to take away

        • Embeddings represent patterns of meaning as numbers. Related texts often score highly, but similarity does not prove factual agreement or that a passage answers your question.
        • Semantic search finds related ideas even when the words differ. For example, “caring for a relative” finds “support for carers”.
        • Keyword search still matters for exact names, codes and acronyms such as “Gift Aid” or “MND”. Many real systems combine both (hybrid search).
        • Embeddings are only as good as the model that made them. General models may not understand specialist medical or legal language well.

        Cosine similarity

        Similarity is usually measured by the angle between two vectors, ignoring their length:

        cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (‖a‖ ‖b‖)      ranges from −1 to 1

        The meaning map uses exactly this on its five-dimensional vectors. Real embedding models produce 384 to 3,072 dimensions, so we can't draw them directly; tools such as UMAP or t-SNE squash them into two dimensions for plotting.

        Real embeddings in Python

        from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer, util

model = SentenceTransformer("all-MiniLM-L6-v2")   # small, free, runs on a laptop
docs  = ["Support for carers looking after a family member",
         "Setting up a monthly direct debit donation"]
query = "help for someone caring for a relative"

scores = util.cos_sim(model.encode(query), model.encode(docs))
print(scores)   # the carers text scores much higher

        Storing embeddings at scale

        A vector database such as Chroma or Qdrant stores embeddings and supports similarity search. FAISS is a similarity-search library rather than a complete database. Approximate indexes can make searches over large collections faster. Keyword search usually uses BM25, a scoring formula that rewards rare words that appear in a document. You'll use a version of it in Lesson 05.
