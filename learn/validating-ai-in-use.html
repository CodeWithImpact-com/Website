Validating AI in use: is it still working?

Testing before launch isn't enough. Data, processes and models change, and quality drifts. Learn how to validate an AI system in use with regular sample checks, honest uncertainty and clear rules for when to act.

Validation is a cycle, not a sign-off

Lesson 07 covered testing before you rely on a system. Validation in use asks a different question: is it still good enough, for this purpose, today? The data a system receives, or the patterns linking its inputs to outcomes, can change. This is called drift. It may happen gradually or suddenly, for example after a new referral form changes the inputs. Performance may change too, so inspect actual outputs. A model update is another reason to revalidate.

Set the bar

Agree acceptance criteria before launch: for example, at least 90% of summaries need no corrections.

Check samples

Every week, a person reviews a random sample of outputs against the source.

Act on evidence

Agree in advance what happens when results fall below the bar.

Key ideas to take away

  • Define “good enough” before launch. Write acceptance criteria tied to the purpose, and who signs them off.
  • Check real outputs regularly. A small random sample, reviewed against the source, is the core of validation in use.
  • Small samples are noisy. Ten checks a week can trigger alerts even when the underlying rate has not changed, or miss a genuine drop. Size the sample to the risk and interpret the alert against your chosen target.
  • Agree the response in advance. Investigate, add extra checks, roll back or pause. Decide before you need to.
  • Revalidate after any change. A new form, data source, template or model version is a reason to check again.

How uncertain is a weekly sample?

If k of n sampled outputs are correct, the lab shows a 95% Wilson interval around k/n:

centre = (p̂ + z²/2n) / (1 + z²/n)
half-width = z·√( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z²/4n² ) / (1 + z²/n)        p̂ = k/n, z = 1.96

With n = 20 and 18 correct, the true rate could plausibly be anywhere from about 70% to 97%. That's why one bad week is a reason to look closer, not proof of failure.

Borrowing from model risk management

Banks have validated models for decades. Their practices translate well: keep an inventory of models (your AI register), validate independently of the people who built or chose the tool, set monitoring thresholds in advance, and record decisions. The PRA's SS1/23 principles are a clear example.

Beyond accuracy

  • Input drift: are the inputs changing (new form, new language, new topic)?
  • Fairness: is quality lower for some groups or reading levels?
  • User feedback: are staff correcting more outputs than before?
