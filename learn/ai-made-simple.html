So what is AI?

Artificial intelligence (AI) is the broad field of getting computers to do tasks that usually need human judgement, such as recognising images, understanding language or making predictions.

Most of the AI you meet today is machine learning: instead of a person writing every rule, a system is shown many examples and learns patterns from them. Show it thousands of emails marked “spam” or “not spam” and it learns which clues, such as particular words, tend to signal spam. These clues are called features. The result of all this learning is called a model: the part that has learned the patterns and makes the predictions.

Generative AI, including tools like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, uses the same idea at a huge scale. It learned patterns from vast amounts of text, and uses them to predict what words are likely to come next. That is why it can write fluently, and also why it can sound confident while being wrong.

Traditional software

A person writes the rules.
“If the email contains prize, mark it spam.”

Machine learning

The system learns the rules from examples.
“Here are 10,000 labelled emails. Find the pattern.”

Generative AI

A model learns patterns from huge collections of text or images, then produces new content. Text models predict the next word; image models use different techniques.

What AI isn't. It isn't thinking or understanding in the way people do. It isn't a database of checked facts. And it isn't automatically up to date: it only knows what was in its training data, plus anything you give it.

Key ideas to take away

  • AI learns patterns from examples. Biased, outdated or mislabelled examples produce a biased, outdated or wrong model.
  • Generative AI predicts plausible content. Plausible is not the same as true, so facts, figures and references always need checking.
  • AI is strongest at drafting, summarising, sorting and searching. It is weakest where accuracy is critical, the stakes for a person are high, or accountability matters.
  • Keep a human in charge. Use AI to support people's judgement, especially in health, safeguarding and decisions about individuals. People should remain responsible for these decisions.

The model in the lab: naive Bayes

For each word w, the model estimates how much more often it appears in urgent messages than in non-urgent ones, with +1 smoothing so unseen words do not produce zeros:

score(w) = log( (count_urgent(w) + 1) / (N_urgent + V) )
         − log( (count_wait(w) + 1)   / (N_wait + V) )

A message's score is the sum of its words' scores plus the log of the prior ratio. Positive means “urgent”. N is the number of words in each class and V the vocabulary size. Words the model has never seen contribute nothing, which is why it struggles with new vocabulary.

The same idea in Python

from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

messages = ["Volunteer fell at the event", "Thanks for the newsletter"]
labels   = ["urgent", "can wait"]

vectoriser = CountVectorizer(stop_words="english")
X = vectoriser.fit_transform(messages)
model = MultinomialNB().fit(X, labels)

print(model.predict(vectoriser.transform(["Venue flooded before the event"])))

Words worth knowing

Training data
The labelled examples a model learns from.
Generalisation
How well a model handles examples it has not seen. Always test on held-out data.
Overfitting
Memorising the training examples instead of learning the underlying pattern.
Foundation model
A large model trained on broad data that can be adapted to many tasks. These models underpin today's generative AI.
