AI in your organisation: safe, legal and genuinely useful.

Three questions before any AI use

A volunteer pastes case notes into a free chatbot. A manager forwards an AI summary without checking the figures. A panel lets a scoring tool reject applicants. None of these needed advanced technology to go wrong. Each one fails one of three simple questions.

What goes in?

Is there personal or sensitive data? Is this tool approved to receive it?

Who is affected?

Could the output change something for a real person: their support, funding or reputation?

Who is accountable?

Which person checks the output and owns the decision?

The rules don't pause for AI. If personal data is involved, UK GDPR (the UK’s main data protection law) applies exactly as it does to a spreadsheet or an email: you need a lawful reason, you should use as little data as possible, tell people what you're doing, and keep it secure.

Key ideas to take away

          
    
            
  • Most AI risk is in everyday use. What gets pasted in, and whether anyone checks what comes out, matter more than which model you choose.
    • 
            
  • Check the tool before the task. Where is the data stored, how long is it kept, and is it used to train the provider's models? Free consumer tools and business accounts often answer these differently.
    • 
            
  • People decide about people. UK law adds safeguards when a significant decision about someone is made without meaningful human involvement, and is stricter still with health data. Use AI to help reviewers, not replace them.
    • 
            
  • Assess high-risk uses first. A data protection impact assessment (DPIA) checks how using personal data could harm people or affect their rights, and how to reduce those risks. It is required before processing likely to create high risks. Health data, vulnerable people and new automated processing are reasons to assess that risk carefully.
    • 
            
  • Write it down and keep it short. A one-page policy, an approved-tools list and a named person to ask prevent most problems.
    •

Data protection principles, applied to AI

              

                
PrincipleWhat it means for AI

                
                  
Lawfulness and transparencyHave a lawful basis, and tell people in your privacy notice that AI is used and how.

                  
Purpose limitationData collected to deliver a service can't simply be reused to train or test a model.

                  
Data minimisationSend the tool the least data that does the job. Remove names and identifiers where you can.

                  
AccuracyAI output about a person is still personal data. Wrong summaries must be corrected.

                  
SecurityUse accounts with proper access controls; know who at the provider can see your data.

                
              

              
Questions to ask an AI supplier

              
    
                
  • Is our data used to train or improve your models? Can we switch that off?
    • 
                
  • Where is it stored and processed, and for how long?
    • 
                
  • Will you sign a data processing agreement?
    • 
                
  • Can we delete our data, and export it if we leave?
    • 
              

              
Automated decisions

              
Since the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, UK law sets out safeguards for significant decisions made solely by automated means: telling the person, letting them make representations, offering human intervention and letting them contest the decision. Using special category data, such as health information, in these decisions is restricted further.

              
If you work with EU partners

              
The EU AI Act sorts AI systems by risk. Most charity uses, such as drafting or summarising, are low risk, but chatbots must make clear people are talking to AI, and uses such as deciding access to essential services or jobs face strict rules. Timelines have been changing, so check the current position if your work reaches EU users.
